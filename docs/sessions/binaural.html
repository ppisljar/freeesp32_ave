Binaural beats

Two steady tones, one in each ear, a few hertz apart. Neither tone pulses. The pulsing you hear is assembled in your own auditory system from the difference between them — which is why it needs headphones, and why you will never find it in a recording.

What the phenomenon is

Play 370 Hz into the left ear and 380 Hz into the right, and most listeners report a slow 10 Hz throb or “rotation” inside the head. The beat rate is exactly the difference between the two carriers. Change either carrier and the beat changes with it; change both by the same amount and the beat does not move at all.

Mechanically, nothing beats. The left ear's cochlea sees a pure 370 Hz tone and the right ear's a pure 380 Hz tone. There is no 10 Hz component anywhere in the acoustic signal, no amplitude modulation in either channel, and no 10 Hz line in a spectrum of either ear's recording. The beat first exists at the point where the two ears' information is combined.

Top: eight cycles of the left-ear carrier. Middle: nine cycles of the right-ear carrier over exactly the same span. Bottom, dashed: the one-cycle beat envelope the listener perceives. It is drawn dashed because it does not exist below the dotted line — not in the file, not in the DAC output, not in a recording. It is the difference between two things that only meet inside the head. The practical consequence for anyone measuring a session: never look for a beat line in a mono sum, because there isn't one. Measure the two carriers separately and subtract.

Mechanism, as currently understood

The honest version is that the perceptual phenomenon is solid and the therapeutic story is not.

What is well supported: binaural beats are audible, the percept depends on binaural integration in the brainstem (the superior olivary complex is the usual candidate), and the percept is strongest for carriers in the low hundreds of hertz with small differences. The commonly cited figures — beat perception peaking around a 400 Hz carrier, falling away badly above about 1 kHz, and requiring a difference under roughly 35 Hz to be heard as a beat rather than as two separate tones — come from the psychoacoustic literature and are the basis for the carrier table in sessions/SESSION_DESIGN_GUIDELINES.md. They are reliable enough to design around.

What is thin: the step from “you hear a 10 Hz beat” to “your cortex is now oscillating at 10 Hz” to “you are therefore in a relaxed/focused/meditative state”. Each of those three steps loses evidence. The entrainment literature for binaural beats specifically is mixed: effect sizes are small, studies are often small and unblinded, null results are common, and the measured cortical steady-state response to a binaural beat is markedly weaker than the response to the same rate delivered as an actual amplitude modulation. That is not surprising given the physics — a binaural beat's effective modulation depth is only around 3 dB, against something close to 100 % for a monaural beat or an isochronic gate. If you want the strongest plausible drive, binaural is the weakest tool in this device's box.

Where that leaves authoring. Binaural's real advantages are that it is gentle — no audible pulsing edge, nothing percussive, nothing that will wake a half-asleep listener — and that it has a distinctive internal spatial quality people like. Those are good reasons to use it for sleep, hypnagogia and long quiet holds. “It drives the cortex hardest” is not one of its advantages; if that is what you want, reach for monaural, isochronic or above all light.

What it is typically used for

  • Sleep onset and delta descents — the absence of any audible edge is the point. Shipped example: 01_sleep_onset.ledc, a 10 → 2 Hz glide over about 18 minutes with a simultaneous light-and-volume soft off and no wake-up.
  • Long steady meditative holds where the design goal is stability rather than stimulation. Shipped example: 17_nondual_awareness.ledc, which holds a 7 Hz beat flat for nineteen and a half minutes and deliberately moves nothing but a slow brightness breath.
  • Dual-frequency “deepening” — a small left/right offset, e.g. 6.0 Hz in one ear's pair and 6.3 Hz in the other's, as in 04_meditation_theta.ledc.
  • Hypnagogic and lucid-dream work, where the listener is close to sleep and an isochronic click would be counter-productive.

Headphones are mandatory, and this is not a style preference. On a speaker the two carriers sum in the air before reaching either ear. You then hear a monaural beat — a real, much deeper amplitude modulation at the same rate, which is a different stimulus with different properties. A binaural session played on a speaker is not a weak binaural session; it is an accidental monaural session. If you want speaker playback, author it as monaural deliberately.

Separately: if your session also flickers an LED anywhere in the 3–30 Hz band, the photosensitivity warning applies in full. See safety.

How to configure it in .ledc

The device supports both binaural idioms, and they are not equivalent.

Idiom A — one channel, using freqR

Set the left carrier in freq and the right carrier in freqR on the same channel. The generator drives the two carriers and routes them straight to the left and right outputs — “binaural bypass”.

#         time  freq  pan  vol  mod  ch  freqR  waveType
A            0   370    0   50    0   1    380         0

That is a 10 Hz beat on channel 1 at a 370 Hz carrier.

pan has no effect on a channel like this. When freqR > 0 and freqR ≠ freq, the pan stage is skipped entirely. This is deliberate: the equal-power pan law would cross-mix the two carriers between the ears, and at any non-centre pan it would attenuate one of them toward zero and collapse the beat. If you need to balance the ears, use two channels, or use vol.

One side effect worth knowing: because the pan law is skipped, a binaural channel is also about 3 dB hotter than a centre-panned mono channel at the same vol — it peaks at −24.1 dBFS rather than −27.1 dBFS. See the mixing model.

Idiom B — two channels, hard-panned

#         time  freq  pan  vol  mod  ch  freqR  waveType
A            0   300 -100   50    0   3      0         0
A            0   307  100   50    0   4      0         0

Both channels go through the normal equal-power pan law, so each ear gets its own carrier with more than 40 dB of separation from the other's. The beat is again the difference, here 7 Hz.

Choosing between the two idioms.
PreferWhenWhy
Idiom A — one channel, freqR Almost always. Every numbered session in the shipped library uses it. Half the channel budget, both carriers guaranteed phase-coherent and level-matched, the beat glidable in one column, and no chance of the two halves drifting apart in later edits.
Idiom B — two hard-panned channels When the ears need to differ in something other than pitch: unequal levels, different timbres (waveType), a different vol envelope per ear, or a per-ear isochronic gate on top. It is the only way to reach pan, per-ear volume, or per-ear pulse settings, because idiom A shares one set of those across both carriers.

Gliding the beat

There are two ways, and one of them is new.

Ramp the left carrier and pin freqR. This is what the shipped library does, and it is the safe choice. freqR is an absolute right-ear frequency, not a detune offset, so moving freq underneath it moves the beat:

A       120000  >370    0   55    0   1    380         0   # beat 10 Hz, ramping
A       420000   374    0   55    0   1    380         0   # beat 6 Hz — the ramp's target

The carrier moves 4 Hz, which is inaudible, while the beat moves from 10 Hz to 6 Hz over five minutes. Note the convention: the > glyph sits on the row where the ramp begins, and the target value is read from the next entry on the same channel.

Ramp freqR directly. As of format revision v3, freqR is a full compound cell and accepts > (linear) and * (quadratic). That makes it the direct way to glide a beat while holding the carrier pitch perfectly still:

A       120000   370    0   55    0   1   >380         0   # right carrier ramping
A       420000   370    0   55    0   1    376         0   # beat 10 -> 6 Hz, carrier never moved

Two cautions on this one. First, freqR accepts > and * only. A periodic modulation glyph (~ ^ / \ _) is accepted by the tokenizer but not implemented: the parser logs a warning, drops the interpolation and holds the start value.

Second, the bundled static linter has not caught up. It still reports LEDC_FREQR_PREFIX at error severity on any freqR carrying a glyph, with the message “freqR cannot be ramped”. That message described a real and nasty bug — a bare atof(">380") evaluated to 0.0, silently collapsing the channel to mono and killing the beat with no diagnostic at all — but the parser now handles the glyph correctly. Until the linter is updated, a session that ramps freqR will not lint clean. If you want a clean lint, use the carrier-ramp idiom above; the behaviour is identical because freqR is absolute.

Restate freqR on any row that moves an audio carrier. An omitted trailing column means “leave this channel's live value unchanged”, and a mono channel's live right carrier equals its left one. So a later row that moves freq and omits freqR leaves the right ear at the old pitch — which then satisfies the binaural-bypass test, so the channel starts playing two different carriers, beats at the size of your carrier change, and silently stops honouring pan. Write freqR explicitly on every audio row that touches freq, using 0 for mono. No lint catches this.

Anti-habituation

A dead-steady beat held for fifteen minutes habituates. The jitter column adds a slow sinusoidal wander, written as <amp_hz> or <amp_hz>:<period_ms>, with the period defaulting to 45 000 ms:

#         time  freq  pan  vol  mod  ch  freqR  wave  duty  env  phase  attack  jitter
A       420000   374    0   55    0   1    380     0    50    4      0       3  0.3:53000

On the audio side jitter is exactly the right tool for binaural and useless for anything else, because the offset is only ever added to the right-ear carrier. Three consequences follow, and all three have cost somebody a measurement session:

  • It is global, not per channel. The last audio line in the file that carries a jitter column sets it for the whole session, on every binaural channel, whatever channel that line addressed.
  • A mono channel never wanders at all — the offset is gated on freqR > 0. Audio jitter on a mono or isochronic channel is a silent no-op.
  • It never touches the isochronic mod rate. To wander a pulse rate, put a periodic modulation on mod instead: ~5.7:6.3:53000.

A period_ms below 1000 is silently ignored — the previous period stays in force. And because the jitter global is not reset between sessions, write it explicitly back to 0 at the end of your file or it leaks into whatever plays next.

Locking light to the beat

Audio and light are locked by writing the same rate in both places, 1:1 — the LED line's freq column and the audio beat. There is no cross-reference mechanism and no “follow” directive; you write the number twice and you keep them in step by hand.

A       420000   374    0   55    0   1    380     0        # audio beat 6 Hz
       420000     6   50   24  255  160    32    15    1  0  # LED flicker 6 Hz, sine gate

Measured audio-versus-light offset on this hardware is a constant latency of roughly 2–3 ms with the audio early, with per-edge scatter of 0.23–0.31 ms. Cross-modal simultaneity tolerance is on the order of 20–40 ms, so that is comfortably imperceptible — the device's microsecond timing is margin, not a parameter you need to manage. A ramp must be written on both lines, each with its own >, or the two will drift apart over the glide.

Complete worked example

binaural_alpha_theta.ledc — a 20-minute alpha-to-theta descent and return, using idiom A with the carrier-ramp glide, with light locked 1:1 and a breathing brightness LFO through the hold. It reports no static problems found under python3 tools/avmeasure/analyze.py lint.

# --- Induction, 10 Hz alpha (00:00-02:00) ---
0 10 50 >0 255 160 32 15 1 0          # amber, sine gate, brightness ramps 0 -> 24
A 0 370 0 >0 0 1 380 0                # ch1 binaural 370/380 = 10 Hz beat; vol ramps 0 -> 55
15000 10 50 24 255 160 32 15 1 0      # brightness reaches the 24% cap (15 s ramp-in)
A 60000 370 0 55 0 1 380 0            # vol settled 55 (60 s fade-in)

# --- Deepen, 10 -> 6 Hz over 5 min (~0.8 Hz/min) (02:00-07:00) ---
A 120000 >370 0 55 0 1 380 0          # left carrier ramps 370 -> 374 => beat 10 -> 6
120000 >10 50 24 255 160 32 15 1 0    # light tracks the beat 1:1, 10 -> 6 Hz

# --- Hold, 6 Hz theta, 10 min (07:00-17:00) ---
A 420000 374 0 55 0 1 380 0           # beat held flat at 6 Hz
420000 6 50 ~18:28:12000 255 160 32 15 1 0   # 0.083 Hz breathing brightness LFO, 18<->28%

# --- Return to alpha (17:00-19:00) ---
A 1020000 >374 0 55 0 1 380 0         # carrier 374 -> 370 => beat 6 -> 10
1020000 >6 50 24 255 160 32 15 1 0    # light 6 -> 10 Hz; static 24% preempts the LFO

# --- Close (19:00-20:00) ---
A 1140000 370 0 >55 0 1 380 0         # volume fades 55 -> 0 over 60 s
1140000 10 50 >24 255 160 32 15 1 0   # brightness fades 24 -> 0 over 60 s
A 1200000 370 0 0 0 1 380 0           # end: silent
1200000 10 50 0 255 160 32 15 1 0     # end: dark

Five things in that file are doing deliberate work:

  1. The carrier is 370 Hz, near the ~400 Hz peak of beat perception, and the beat is 10 Hz, well inside the ~35 Hz limit.
  2. Every row restates freqR — including the rows that only change volume — so there is no way for the accidental-binaural trap to fire.
  3. The glide is about 0.8 Hz/min. The frequency-following response can only track a slow change; 0.25–2 Hz/min is the working range and anything above ~3 Hz/min is worth a second look.
  4. The return to a static 24 at 17:00 pre-empts the LFO before the fade starts, so the final brightness ramp has a known, honest start value. A ramp on a running channel starts from the live value, so a ramp beginning while an LFO is mid-swing is a ramp whose literal is lying to the reader.
  5. The light and audio fades are simultaneous and the file ends at zero on both.

Full column semantics, including everything the - sentinel does, are on the reference page; pacing, testing and the trap list are in the authoring guide.
