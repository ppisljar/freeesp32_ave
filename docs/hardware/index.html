Hardware builder guide

The firmware supports two chip builds: the ESP32-S3 board with a TI TLV320DAC3101 codec, which is where development happens, and the classic ESP32 boards, which still build and run. This page is the pin-level record of both, taken from the config snapshots and drivers in the repository rather than from memory.

Flicker safety

Everything on this page exists to flash light at the eyes. Before you build or power anything, read the photosensitivity and epilepsy warning — flicker in the 3–30 Hz band is a real seizure trigger, the 15–25 Hz region is the risk peak, and the firmware enforces no limit of its own.

The two builds at a glance

Four board snapshots exist (sdkconfig.glasses, sdkconfig.ac101, sdkconfig.es8388, sdkconfig.yb_s3_dac), selected with ./switch_board.sh <board>. Three of them are the same chip; only the S3 snapshot changes the toolchain target, so switching to or from it fully reconfigures the build directory.

Why the two targets are separate code, not one tunable

Divergences are deliberately kept as per-target modules selected by IDF_TARGET in main/CMakeLists.txt, and every one of them is catalogued in legacyesp32.md, which doubles as the removal checklist for the day classic support is dropped. The LED driver is the clearest case: the S3's non-DMA RMT path would actually be worse than the classic chip's (192 versus 512 symbols of FIFO), so it is DMA that makes the S3 better, not the peripheral — which is exactly why it cannot be one file with a constant in it.

Audio output, and the gain choices behind it

The codec is a paged register device: register 0x00 on every page is PAGE_SELECT, and playback init touches page 0 (clocks, I2S format, DAC power, digital volume) and page 1 (analog routing, output drivers, analog volume). Three choices in that driver are deliberate and worth understanding before you change anything:

  • The codec PLL runs from BCLK, not MCLK. Because JP2 ships open the chip receives no MCLK at all, so the driver clocks its PLL from BCLK — which is also what the board vendor's own shipping example does. BCLK is Fs × 32 here, because I2S is configured as 16-bit stereo. Only set an MCLK pin if you have actually closed that bridge.
  • It wants Philips I2S framing, not the left-justified framing the other supported codecs use. audio_manager.c selects this per codec.
  • The class-D gain is held low on purpose. The gain field is two bits with four values (6 / 12 / 18 / 24 dB). It sits at 12 dB — raised one step from the 6 dB minimum at the owner's request, which is +6 dB and four times the electrical power, because the field cannot express the 9 dB that was actually wanted. TI's datasheet example uses 24 dB. This amp can deliver 1.3 W into 8 Ω at full tilt, which will destroy the small speakers typically wired to the JST connectors. Set listening level with the digital DAC volume and the −9 dB analog stage, both reversible from the web UI; if you ever need a finer step than the coarse field allows, the speaker analog volume is the 0.5 dB lever on that path.

Two classic-chip constraints shape those numbers. MCLK can only be routed to GPIO 0, 1 or 3 — a hard pin-mux limit, which is why the ES8388 boards are stuck with MCLK on GPIO0 when they use it at all; the S3 has no such restriction. And the es8388 snapshot can legitimately drive LEDs on GPIO 16 and 17 precisely because that board has no PSRAM, which on a PSRAM-equipped board those pins would be part of.

LED topology and per-eye masks

The engine exposes eight logical LED channels, addressed by a bitmask in the session file where bit 0 is LED1. A channel is not a pixel: CONFIG_LED_CHANNEL_MAP assigns a channel index to every physical pixel, so a channel is a zone of arbitrary shape. The eye builds populate four of the eight — two zones per eye.

The eye split was established by measurement rather than by reading the harness: each channel was driven at its own distinct rate in one session and each eye's photocell spectrum then named the channels it could see, with 30–43 dB of separation. The result is unambiguous — {LED1, LED2} is one eye and {LED3, LED4} is the other, so the per-eye masks are 3 and 12.

Measuring an antiphase session needs one sensor per eye

A photocell that can see both an inner and an outer zone measures the residual of a near-cancellation, not the flicker. On a verified antiphase session the two sensors read fundamentals 25.8 dB apart with a relative phase of +178.1° against a commanded 180 — the design working, not a fault — and per-cycle timing analysis of the cancelling channel becomes impossible. Point each sensor at one zone.

Form factors: glasses and lamp

The chip build and the physical build are independent choices. Two end products are documented:

Glasses (wearable)

LEDs mounted in front of the eyes behind a diffuser, audio through the board's headphone jack, powered from USB or a battery. Uses the NeoPixel backend on a single data pin. This is the build both pin maps above describe, and the one the session library is authored for.

Lamp (room device)

A high-power COB emitter aimed at the room instead of the eyes, plus speaker output; mains-powered and fixed in place. Uses the Direct LEDC PWM backend: the ESP32 emits only a 0–100% PWM control signal at 25 kHz into a constant-current LED driver's dimming input, and the driver does all the high-current switching. A dual-channel warm/cold COB pair gives four driver channels, one per LED_DIRECT_PIN_CH1..CH4.

Assembly order

  1. Solder leads to the emitters. Four pads each: warm +/− and cold +/−. Silicone-insulated 22 AWG handles 1.4 A per channel and survives the iron. Tin pad and wire separately, then join quickly — COBs do not enjoy being heated.
  2. One driver per channel. Driver +OUT/−OUT to the matching channel pair. Keep runs under 20 cm. All four drivers share the +12 V input rail; each keeps its own dimming line.
  3. Dimming lines to four GPIOs, defaults LED_DIRECT_PIN_CH1..CH4 = 12, 13, 14, 15, plus a shared ground between the drivers' dimming lines and the ESP32. Build with the Direct LEDC backend.
  4. Stack the optics: heatsink (fan facing out) → thermal paste → COB → diffuser lens. Space the two emitters for an even field at the distance someone will actually sit.
  5. Wire the buck from the 12 V rail to the board's 5 V input — after trimming it to 5.0 V.
  6. Mains last: 220 V adapter → 12 V rail → splits to the four drivers and the buck. Fuse the 12 V side.
  7. Flash and test before closing the case. Exercise each channel on its own with a .ledc using mask 1, 2, 4 and 8 in turn, and confirm all four respond.

No USB cable is needed in normal operation — the buck feeds the board from the same rail as the LEDs. The three hazards this build adds are below.

The lamp build adds three hazards the glasses build does not have
  • Mains. The AC side must live in an insulated enclosure with strain relief on the cord and no exposed conductors, and the DC rail wants an inline fuse sized around 1.5× expected draw.
  • Emitter brightness. A 100 W COB is intense enough to damage eyes. Never look at the bare emitter without its diffuser, even briefly.
  • Heat. The heatsink runs at 60–90 °C in operation. Active cooling is required at 100 W; passive is feasible to roughly 50 W with a large finned sink.

Power

  • Glasses, USB. A 5 V USB supply or power bank feeds the board, and the LED boards share that 5 V rail and ground. A 1000 mAh-plus bank runs a session comfortably. Keep the USB port reachable through the frame so you can re-flash without disassembly.
  • Glasses, battery. A single 18650 Li-ion cell with a protection board, or AA/AAA cells through a boost converter. Whether the cell can connect directly depends on the board: some audio boards have a JST-PH battery input and an onboard charger, others do not. Check your board's documentation rather than assuming.
  • Lamp, mains. A 220 V AC adapter produces a 12 V DC rail that splits between the constant-current LED drivers and a 12 V → 5 V buck converter feeding the ESP32 board. Trim the buck's output to exactly 5.0 V before connecting it — over-voltage destroys the board. Do not feed 12 V to a 3.3 V input; most ESP32 dev boards expect 5 V upstream of their own regulator.
  • Signal-wire note. On LED data runs longer than about 10 cm, a 470 Ω resistor in series with DIN helps signal integrity. For a driver's dimming input, remember to share ground between the driver and the ESP32.

Hazards worth knowing before you power up

GPIOs the firmware refuses outright

Pin assignments are runtime settings reachable over an unauthenticated POST /api/settings, and the IDF helper GPIO_IS_VALID_GPIO answers "does this pad exist on this die", not "is this pad free" — so the pads bonded to the SPI flash and the PSRAM pass it. Assigning one of those is not a bad setting, it is a remote brick: the value reaches NVS before the reboot that applies it, no driver stops it (RMT, LEDC, I2S and I2C each log a warning about a reserved pin and then mux it anyway), and a board that cannot read its own flash cannot be talked out of it over the air — it needs USB and a boot button. Nothing legitimate wants those pins, so the firmware refuses them.

Two details make that guard non-obvious. The reserved-pin mask has to be snapshotted at boot, before any driver starts, because the same mask is what drivers use to record "I own this pad now" — validated live it would reject the very pin map the device is currently running on. And on this particular S3 board the snapshot has a hole: the PSRAM data lines D4..DQS are driven on every PSRAM access but are absent from the mask, because IDF only reserves the MSPI data lines when the flash is octal, and here DIO flash sits next to OPI PSRAM. They are patched in from the SoC's own pin header. There is deliberately no classic-ESP32 equivalent — there the quad-PSRAM driver reserves the full set at runtime.

Pin defaults that are claims on real pins

A default written for one board is not inert on another, and a collision does not fail loudly — it degrades the peripheral that was already there. This cost hours twice during S3 bring-up:

  • button_gpio defaulted to 5, which on the YB-ESP32-S3-DAC is the I2S bit clock. Configuring it as a button input degraded BCLK just enough that the codec still locked its PLL — a PLL averages over many cycles — but could never latch a sample. The symptom was perfect clocks, a working internal beep, a verified data line, and total silence.
  • sd_cs defaulted to 5 too, because the SD pin symbols used to be gated on the SD feature being enabled. With SD off the symbols did not exist, so the seed fell through to hardcoded classic-board pins. Those gates are gone: pins must be seedable even when the feature is off.

settings_init() now runs a conflict check that logs an ERROR naming both claimants of any shared GPIO. It only warns — an inactive backend's pins are harmless — but it turns this failure mode into one line in the boot log. Read that line.

The codec that answers nothing

On the S3 board GPIO21 is wired to the codec's ~RESET through a solder bridge that ships closed, and the pin comes up floating — which holds the codec in reset so it ACKs nothing. The whole I2C bus then reads as empty. It presents as "no I2C devices found", which looks exactly like a wiring fault, and a software reset over I2C cannot help because it needs working I2C to begin with. The driver pulses the pin before the first transaction; if you move the jumper to its other side, set AUDIO_CODEC_RESET_GPIO to -1.

Speaker power versus speaker size

Covered in Build A above, and worth repeating as a hazard: 1.3 W into 8 Ω from the class-D amp will destroy the small speakers usually wired to this board's JST connectors, and these boards are typically used right next to someone's head. The coarse gain is at 12 dB of a possible 24. Raise it only after checking what your speakers can take.

Flashing and first boot

The first deploy must be over USB, because OTA cannot change a partition table. After that, app updates go over the air. 

source ./activate.sh              # ESP-IDF v5.5.2 environment
./switch_board.sh yb_s3_dac       # or glasses | ac101 | es8388
idf.py build
./flash_all.sh --port /dev/tty.usbserial-XXXX

flash_all.sh is the only thing that lays down the whole layout: bootloader, partition table, the otadata seed, the main app into ota_0, the standalone minimal OTA updater into ota_1, and the web UI into the storage SPIFFS partition. It derives every offset at run time from the build rather than hardcoding them — which matters, because the bootloader offset is 0x1000 on the classic ESP32 and 0x0 on the S3 — and it re-targets the updater project to the same chip, since a wrong-chip updater in ota_1 would leave the board unable to recover over the air. Use --dry-run to see the esptool command without flashing.

One build warning is expected, not a fault

idf.py build prints a standing Part 'ota_1' ... too small for binary on both targets. ota_1 deliberately holds the minimal OTA updater, not a second copy of the app, so it is sized for the updater. Both partition tables use that same dual-boot model.

First boot

  1. With no Wi-Fi configured, the device raises a SoftAP named ESP32-AVE-Setup (WPA2 password entrain123). Connect and browse to http://192.168.4.1.
  2. Set your SSID and password in Settings. On the next boot it joins as a station and advertises itself over mDNS, so http://esp32-ave.local reaches it whatever address DHCP handed out. The S3 snapshot seeds a different name, esp32-ave-mobile.local, deliberately — two devices claiming one .local name knock each other offline.
  3. Confirm the LED boards light on boot, and check the log for the pin-conflict ERROR line described above.
  4. To force setup mode later, hold the configured button during power-on or reset. It is active-low against the chip's internal pull-up. On a board where the button is -1, that escape hatch does not exist — recover over USB.

Afterwards: ./ota_flash.sh <ip> pushes a new app over Wi-Fi, and ./flash_web.sh rebuilds the web UI and flashes only the storage partition. The web UI lives in SPIFFS, not in the app image, so OTA alone leaves the device serving the old bundle.

Measuring what you built

A build is not finished when it lights up. The things that decide whether a session does what it claims — how far the light lags the sound, whether a 10 Hz flicker is 10 Hz, whether two eyes are in phase or antiphase — depend on your wiring, your LED backend and your diffuser, and none of them are visible by looking. They are, however, cheap to record. Everything on measured results came off the rig below.

Building it

  1. Solder a 100 nF capacitor in series with one leg of each panel, and the panel's two leads to a jack. Keep the two channels identical: any difference between them shows up as a fake inter-eye offset.
  2. Tape one panel over each eye cup, facing the LEDs, as close to where an eye would be as the build allows. Note which LED zone each panel sits over — the analyser's channel map needs it, and the sensor's position along the LED chain is itself worth writing down (below).
  3. Plug each into its own input and set that input to Hi-Z / instrument, not line.
  4. Power the device from an isolated USB adapter, not from the same computer as the interface.
  5. Check the floor before trusting anything: record a few seconds with the LEDs dark and look at 50 Hz (or 60 Hz). On this rig the line sits at −87.6 dBFS, giving a sensor signal-to-noise of +45.2 dB and +41.8 dB.

Why those two choices matter

Hi-Z, not line. On a line input (~20 kΩ) the series capacitor differentiates — corner around 80 Hz — and the capture shows edge spikes instead of a brightness envelope. The harmonic ladder of a 50 % square flattens to 1 / 0.91 / 0.86 / 0.82 / 0.79, and every amplitude or brightness number becomes meaningless. On a high-impedance input the same signal reads 1 / 0.33 / 0.20 / 0.14 / 0.11, matching theory to within 0.01. If your interface has no instrument input, the rig will produce confident, wrong numbers rather than obviously broken ones.

Isolate the device's USB. Sharing a ground with the recording computer put a 10 Hz LED component into the audio channels at +44 dB — which looked exactly like a firmware crosstalk bug and was chased as one. On an isolated adapter it drops to +16.9 dB, which is 39.6 dB below the audio carrier and not worth chasing.

A solar cell is a convenient photodiode. Large area, no bias supply, no amplifier, output at a level an instrument input is happy with, and bandwidth orders of magnitude beyond the 100 Hz ceiling of anything this device emits. It also has the polarity you want: more light means a more positive voltage, so the waveform rises when the LED turns on. A phototransistor with a pull-up, or a photodiode into a transimpedance amp, both pull down on light — tell the analyser with a ! before the channel number (--map "light1=!3") rather than rewiring.

Taking a capture

Start recording, play a session, stop. The one thing to get right is where the session's t=0 sits in the recording: the recorder started when you pressed record, the session started when the device accepted the POST, and nothing in the file relates the two. A wrong t=0 makes every timing number downstream meaningless. Author a sync marker into the .ledc and the analyser finds it; otherwise it cross-correlates and tells you how much it trusts the result.

Two sensors is the useful minimum — one per eye also gives inter-eye phase, which is the only way to confirm an antiphase pair really is in antiphase. The analyser degrades honestly with less: with no light sensors the audio-to-light offset is reported as not measurable rather than guessed, and with one audio channel the binaural beat comes back BEAT_UNAVAILABLE.

The channel map, the sync methods, what each number means and which survive a dropped buffer are in tools/avmeasure/README.md, with the practical cautions summarised under how to test a session. What the rig has actually produced is on measured results.

Making your own variant

The firmware is built to be re-pinned rather than forked. Every codec driver and all three LED backends are compiled into a single image, and the active one is a runtime setting; the Kconfig options only seed NVS on first boot or after a settings reset. So in many cases a new board needs no code at all — just the Settings page.

  1. Start from the closest snapshot. Copy the sdkconfig.<board> that matches your chip and codec, give it your own name, and edit the pin seeds. If you are bringing up a new chip target, add a sdkconfig.defaults.<target> too — ESP-IDF loads it automatically and it is the right home for flash size, PSRAM mode and the partition table.
  2. Check every pin against the chip, then against the board. The classic ESP32 has holes at 24 and 28–31 and is input-only above 33; the S3 has holes at 22–25. Beyond that, a pin can exist and still be spoken for by flash, PSRAM, or another peripheral on your board.
  3. Lay out the zones with LED_CHANNEL_MAP. It is a comma-separated channel index per pixel, as long as LED_COUNT; pixels past the end of the list fall back to channel 0. This is where a physical strip becomes logical zones, so it is also where a geometry change lands.
  4. Keep the mirrored eye convention. Number the zones inner/outer on one eye and outer/inner on the other, as described in LED topology. Every shipped session's use of mask 9 and mask 6 depends on it; break it and the two eyes do opposite things.
  5. Verify the map by measurement, not by inspection. Drive each channel at a distinct rate in one session and read the spectrum at each eye. That identifies the whole map in a single capture and needs no knowledge of how the strip was wired — which is exactly how the current map was established.
  6. For a direct-PWM build, set LED_DIRECT_PIN_CH1..CH8 to the GPIOs going to your driver dimming inputs, and use LED_DIRECT_ACTIVE_LOW_MASK for channels where driving the pin high turns the LED off — common on integrated audio dev boards. Test each channel individually with a session that exercises mask=1, 2, 4, 8 in turn.

What this page could not establish

Four things are recorded here as open rather than guessed at, because the source does not settle them:

  • LED5 produces no light either sensor can see (at the noise floor on both). No shipped session uses a mask above 15, so a four-zone build is consistent and the channel is probably simply unpopulated — but that is an inference, not a confirmation.
  • LED6 is visible to one eye at about 8 dB below LED1/LED2, despite never being addressed by any shipped session. If channels map onto ranges of one physical pixel chain, channel 6 may alias onto pixels in that eye. Unverified.
  • Inner versus outer within an eye rests on the wiring, not on measurement. A single sensor per eye established the eye split conclusively but cannot separate the inner zone from the outer one inside that eye. The ring assignment comes from the channel map's own comments.
  • The es8388 snapshot and its driver disagree about MCLK. The Kconfig help and the driver both state that the ES8388 requires MCLK from the ESP32 (GPIO0 on the classic chip, the only pins MCLK can reach being 0, 1 and 3), yet sdkconfig.es8388 ships AUDIO_I2S_MCLK_GPIO=-1. One of the two is stale. If you are bringing up an ES8388 board and get silence, this is the first thing to check.

Likewise, the project README describes the original reference design as a XIAO ESP32-S3 board and points at sdkconfig.glasses for its pin map, but that snapshot is a classic-ESP32 configuration. The pin values in the Build B table above are what the file actually contains; treat the board attribution in the README as unresolved.
